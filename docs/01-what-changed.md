# 01: What Changed, and Why Screens Need Checking Now

Faking media isn't new — doctored photos are older than the smell of darkroom
fixer. So why a manual now? Because three things changed at once, and
together they moved the job of checking from "occasionally, for journalists"
to "often, for everyone."

## What actually got faster: three things

1. **The making got almost free.** A convincing image used to need a
   Photoshop expert and an afternoon. Now it's a sentence, ten seconds, and
   one free tool. Voices need about thirty seconds of a person talking
   (social media is full of it). Video clips take a little longer, and
   that's improving too.
2. **The distribution got enormous.** When anyone with a family group chat
   can reach two hundred people instantly, the filter "a newspaper decided
   whether this is worth showing you" is gone. There's no editor between an
   AI image and your aunt. You — and your five minutes — are the editor now.
3. **The old tells died.** Grainy photos used to signal something.
   Broken grammar in a post used to signal something. AI generation gives
   us high-res, correctly punctuated fakes, so the tells moved to places
   most people never zoom into: hands, reflections, light, seams, and
   metadata.

None of this means the internet is mostly fake. Most images you see are
real. But the *share-your-emotion* category — the amazing, the adorable,
the outrageous — is now thoroughly polluted, and that's exactly the content
people forward fastest.

## Where fakes actually show up (the honest list)

Real headlines don't usually travel as "what!! no way!!" images. The
voluntary spending of attention is, in practice:

- **"Incredible" nature, weather, and animals.** Two-headed snapping
  turtles, spiraling auroras that no one saw anywhere else, giant produce.
- **Sweet and political "facts" in screenshots.** Made-up posts from real
  commentators with letters missing from the handle, fake replies, fake
  notifications.
- **"Leaked audio" and odd voice notes.** The always-funny celebrity
  voice-over meme (fine, harmless comedy) and the unverified "you won't
  believe what [name] said" clip (less fine).
- **Breaking-news video.** Every major storm, crash, or explosion generates
  both real footage and generated footage of the *same kind of event*.
- **Cute, harmless-looking AI "art."** Most is shared by people who have no
  idea it's generated — AI can look like a tiny adorable hedgehog in a
  raincoat because the smell of a hedgehog in a raincoat checks out.
  Innocent sharing, still worth labeling what it is.
- **Ads and "memes" with subtle wrongness.** Including the almost-real
  political-quote-near-miss, which is the harvest of chapter 03.

## A word the internet needs: the "shallowfake"

Not every fake is fabricated pixels. A huge amount of damage comes from
**real media with a false story attached** — a 2019 flood shown as "yesterday,"
a different country's protest labeled as "ours," a real screenshot with a
new caption. Detectives call this kind of thing something fancy; call it a
**shallowfake** — the pixels are real and the frame around them is a lie.

That matters enormously here, for two reasons:

1. **Reverse image search is as good against shallowfakes as it is against
   deepfakes.** Finding the original 2019 post fixes the 2026 caption.
2. **The old "I trust what I saw" instinct fails on shallowfakes.** The
   pixels *are* worth believing; it's the story wrapped around them that
   needs the tutorial. (Chapter 05 adds a slot for the shallowfake checks.)

## The crying-wolf problem (the quiet new harm)

Here's the chapter's most important paragraph. When a disaster photo goes
viral, someone — often genuinely affected, sometimes a journalist — posted a
real photo. And then comment sections fill up with "AI!!" and "fake, this is
generated," because a lot of these posts are generated. Both things are true:
real disaster photos get false-flagged as AI, *and* fake ones slip through
labeled real.

Getting this wrong damages real people (imagine your flooded house being
told it's "AI slop") and, over time, trains everyone to ignore all
documentation. This guide's whole approach is built to protect against both
mistakes — over-believing and over-accusing — and it does that with one
sensible move:

> Don't give a verdict quickly, even in your head. Give it after the
> check, or land on "unresolved" and move on.

## The new bare minimum: strong emotion, slow share

If you only take one thing from this whole chapter: **the stronger the
emotion, the slower you should share.** It works like an inverse lever:
heartfelt + astonishing + angry = hold, press, verify. Mundane + helpful +
from a friend you trust directly = probably fine, be a human.
