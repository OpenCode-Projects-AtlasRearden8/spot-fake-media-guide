# 04: Cloned Voices and "Leaked Audio"

The voice is rolling a new part these days: a voice note in chat, an
"unreleased recording" shared by your most allergic-to-news friend, a public
figure doing a set, a robocall in a familiar voice. Thirty seconds of any
one speaking — livestream, a podcast, you sending a voice memo to say
"meet plan C" — is enough for current cloning tools to generate new speech
in that voice. The old instinct "it sounds exactly like them" fails, and
will fail for a while. This chapter is about listening *at* a recording,
not to it.

(The live-phone-call version — someone calls you, sounds like family, asks
for help — is covered exhaustively in *The AI Scam Safety Guide*, chapter
04, "Cloned-Voice Calls," including the family passphrase and the
hang-up-and-call-back drill. Do the drill. This chapter is about clips,
which live in a slightly different world.)

## Listening-pass-the-smoothness tells

Real speech is messy in characteristic ways. Fake speech is clean in
characteristic ways. Listen for:

1. **Breath and mouth.** Real recordings breathe — audibly, at ends of
   phrases — plus throat clears, lip smacks, the little "um" before a
   proper name. Clones often drop breaths entirely, or place a breath at a
   place a human wouldn't (in the middle of a word, say), with a rhythmic
   evenness that feels more like a metronome than a mammal.
2. **The pause test (slow it down if you can).** Most apps can play at
   half speed. At 0.5x, cloned speech often develops little flat patches —
   wordless pauses between words where a human would slide. Real speech
   at half speed still sounds like a person in molasses; clone speech
   sounds like words being loaded one at a time.
3. **Prosody, the music.** Listen to where the stresses land. Cloning
   models occasionally get the tune odd: emphasis on the wrong word ("I
   definitely did NOT say that... *did* say"), the upswing of a question on
   a statement, or a strange sing-song loop that repeats across sentences.
   Emotion that doesn't match — flat tones for a joyful announcement, or
   high energy at unnatural hold-points — is a strong tell.
4. **Word-shaped errors.** Cloned speech is usually *too* fluent; but the
   failure mode when it stumbles is "word salad": a plausible-looking
   sentence that breaks its own logic mid-way ("before the meeting — of the
   meeting — we will meet"). Also listen for mispronounced names: AI
   clones struggle with names, and public figures' names are exactly what
   these clips claim to be about.
5. **The room.** Every real recording happens somewhere: room echo, hiss,
   the hum of a fridge, distant traffic that continues *through* the clip.
   Fake clips often have backgrounds that are too quiet, or a background
   that doesn't intelligently change when the person supposedly changes
   rooms. A clip presented as "secretly recorded at a restaurant" that
   sounds like a sound-booth is wearing a costume two sizes wrong.
6. **The join seams.** If a clip was edited (or assembled from pieces),
   listen at the cuts: the background hiss changes color at the edit point;
   a word that begins at a slightly different loudness than the sentence
   around it; an applause that cuts recklessly. Headphones or a car's
   speakers, hand on the volume: turn it up; seams hide at low volume.

Two cuts of caution:

- These tells are *a list of suspects*, not a court. Slip into the habit
  of finding **two** before rising toward "fake."
- Good clone detection is genuinely hard — for humans *and* for software.
  Recognize that a clip can pass every sound check and still be a clone;
  good clones exist at every quality level. The checks below beat the
  tells eventually.

## The two checks that beat every tell

1. **The un-Googleable question.** If the clip claims to be someone you
   know (or someone reachable), ask them something a recording can't know:
   "where did we eat last Christmas?", "what color was the jacket you wore
   last weekend?" — any specific personal memory, asked out of nowhere.
   Clones answer scripts, not lives. (If this is a live call, the fuller
   version of this defense is the family passphrase — (*The AI Scam Safety
   Guide*, chapter 04).)
2. **The second-source rule.** A "leaked audio" clip is treated here the
   way newsrooms treat tips: a single recording is a claim, not news. Does
   a second, independent source — a named outlet quoting the recording with
   an explanation of where it got it, a second recording, a document,
   people named in it acknowledging it — corroborate it? If not, your
   verdict stays "unverified," not "true" and not "false."

## The genre guide (what you're probably listening to)

| The clip is... | Mostly it is... | The move |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A celebrity "singing" or joking in a meme | Deliberately fake, advertised by taste | Enjoy, but never forward *as quotes*; caption is where fakes get serious |
| A "leaked" clip of a public figure | Unverified rumor | Second-source rule; wait for named outlets |
| An emergency robocall sounding like an official | Deliberate (scam-shaped) | Hang up; the organization's real number is the one you look up |
| A voice note from a friend asking for something odd | Could be the phone-call scam in chat clothing | The un-Googleable question first |

One privacy-habit note worth repeating far ahead of when you need it: every
minute of public audio of you is raw clone material. Livestreaming a
birthday, posting long voicemails, public interviews — fine to share life,
just know the trade. You're not required to disappear; you're just not
required to hand out the key either. (The privacy side of this lives in
*The Privacy Comfort Handbook*, another sibling in the same series.)

## The one-line lift for your family

> "If a voice can say anything, the voice stops being proof — so now we
> verify identities by plan, not by ear."
