# 11: Practice Scenes (Five Little Mysteries)

Reading about spotting is 20% of spotting — the rest is reps. These five
scenes mimic real media you'll meet. For each one: pause, decide what you'd
check first, then expand the answer and see how close you got. (All scenes
are fictional composites; any resemblance to a specific viral post is a
coincidence, though a sadly predictable one.)

## Scene 1: The gorgeous "abandoned hotel pool in the Alps"

An aerial photo: turquoise water in a marble hotel pool, snowcapped peaks,
one perfect swimsuit on the deck. Thirty thousand likes. The caption says
"this REAL abandoned hotel is still for sale — I'm tempted." Your friend
asks whether you want to go in on it.

**What you'd check first?**

:::details Answer: Scene 1
Zoom habit, chapter 02: the pool *tiles* have a repeating pattern that
doesn't survive corner geometry (marble lines bending where physics
says straight); one swimsuit strap fades INTO tile; the shadow of the
diving board points a different direction from the mountain shadows; and
the waterline reflection shows different mountains than the ones standing
above it. Text check: zoomed all the way, the hotel sign spells "HOTL
SPARKT."

Verdict path: two-plus tells → reverse image search (chapter 06) → the
oldest match is a generative-AI artist's page from two years ago with the
prompt thread included. Verdict: **fake (AI), trail documented** — share
it as art *if labeled*, never as a real property. (And "famous hotel,
unbeatable price" drifts toward too-good-deal territory anyway —
*The AI Scam Safety Guide*, chapter 06, owns that conversation.)
:::

## Scene 2: The disaster photo "from your hometown, just now"

After a big storm: a photo of a collapsed cable bridge, blurry and
low-resolution, no words on it. It's posted in your family chat with "someone just
sent me this from home!!" Your aunt is from a town with a famous bridge —
and part of it falling would be exactly the story that matches.

**What you'd check first?**

:::details Answer: Scene 2
The two shallowfake questions from chapter 05 apply before any pixel-peek:
When (dates besides the post) and where (town?). Reverse-search the frame
(chapter 06's keyframe move): oldest match is a wire-agency photo page
from 2021 — a bridge in another country, one that, in fact, later got
rebuilt. The pixels are real; the caption is fake.

The verdict is the special one (chapter 10): **real pixels, false story.**
Reply with Script 2 from chapter 09 — a kind correction with the agency
link, and maybe an actual news link about the storm that did hit today, so
the chat gets both truth and news relief.
:::

## Scene 3: The screenshot "receipt" of a celebrity saying the thing

A screenshot: what looks like a well-known commentator's post, saying
something far more extreme than they have ever said publicly. It's hot in
the group chat, 4 replies of agreement and growing.

**What you'd check first?**

:::details Answer: Scene 3
Chapter 03's checklist, quickly. The pasted-line seam — the "post text"
reads a half-step darker and its baseline sits two pixels lower relative to
its neighbors. The handle has a typo: the underscore is missing. Clock
math: the post's timestamp reads 3:41 AM while the status bar of the very
same screenshot says 4:02 PM — the classic pasted-clock mismatch.

Fastest move: search the exact text in quotes (chapter 03, check #1) →
no exact phrase matches, but paraphrase-matches a near-miss "quote" from a
parody account, which itself is one letter off the real handle. The verdict:
**fake screenshot** (and the "quote" audit trail points to a parody page).

What you do: kind correction with a link to the commentator's real, live
account, plus one line — "and this is why I just quote-check before fun
rants." Optional ten seconds: report the fake (chapter 10).
:::

## Scene 4: The "leaked audio" clip of a mayor

A clip circulating with grainy audio: a mayor "caught saying" something
scandalous about a new housing plan. The person sharing it says "this is
recorded from last week." The clip is 26 seconds long, background always
silent, and the speaker never once takes a breath.

**What you'd check first?**

:::details Answer: Scene 4
Chapter 04's listening tells. No breath, ever, across 26 seconds — a
mammal-shaped little bit of impossibility. The background is dead flat in
a clip claimed to be "recorded at a public meeting" (no room, no other
voices, no chair scrape when the "meeting" is allegedly raucous). One
breath appears at second 19 — exactly at a word boundary and nowhere a
human would inhale, because a human inhales mid-sentence when out of
air.

Second-source rule: no named outlet has the recording or describes the
event in the 4 days since "last week." Verdict: **unverified** — and you
say exactly that ("unresolved" is legitimate; chapter 10). Waiting calmly
for corroboration is not being passive — impatience is exactly the product
the people who make these clips are selling.
:::

## Scene 5: The video of a politician at a rally

A vertical clip: a familiar public figure at a podium rally, saying something
glass-shattering. The clip is 11 seconds, re-encoded-to-death, camera
moving, and the crowd roars. Your friend says there's "a longer version,"
that "it's coming," that "they all deleted their posts."

**What you'd check first?**

:::details Answer: Scene 5
Chapter 05, in order. Lip-sync check on labials: at second 3, "my people" —
the lips never close for the "m"; the teeth flicker oddly on the "th."
The hairline: at the wind moment, a shimmer of pixels around the
ear while the head turns. The hands: during the gesture at second 7, the
wedding ring blinks between a band and nothing. Then the loop's slow-motion
trick: at 0.25x you can *see* the jaw swimming against the words.

Claim-vs-resolution check: an 11-second clip carrying a glass-shattering
claim, no longer version anywhere, is the exact range fakes are comfortable
in — so, search the keyframe. The scoreboard at second 2 finds the rally's
original livestream from six months earlier, and the sentence turns out to
be cut from between two other sentences, reshaping the phrase entirely
(real pixels, bad edit, out-of-context quote — a shallowfake all the same).

Verdict: **real video, false context, and probably not even a
full-fake face — an out-of-context edit.** The correction might genuinely
defend the politician just *this once*, which is one of the soft
education moments of this whole guide: there are no teams in this, only
truth, and you just won one for a person you might disagree with next
week. That's the job.
:::

## Make your own set (the family upgrade)

Once you've run the five scenes, invent a sixth: everyone in the family
grabs one piece of media they *actually* get sent this week, checks it
(chapter 10's loop), and brings the case to dinner. Rules for the table:

- No shame — the person "fooled" becomes the person who taught
  three people the check.
- Always lead with what the check found, not with "you're wrong."
- Celebrate "unresolved," too: "I looked; I couldn't verify either way"
  is a first-place finish, not a participation ribbon.

A family that practices one story a week is nearly fraud-proofed by the
time the snow melts — and it's more fun than it has any right to be.
