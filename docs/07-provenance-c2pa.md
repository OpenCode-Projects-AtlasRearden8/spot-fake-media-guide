# 07: Provenance: When Content Carries Its Receipts

Here's the good chapter. Not everything on the internet is a blind guess.

An increasing amount of media — real photos from professional cameras,
images and video generated by major AI tools, edited content — now carries
a **provenance receipt**: a signed, tamper-evident record attached to the
file that says where it was "born" and what was done to it since. It's the
digital-age version of a carfax for pixels, and learning to read it is a
five-minute skill that will pay off for years.

## C2PA and Content Credentials, in plain English

The main receipt system is a standard called **C2PA** (the name of the tech
behind it; "Content Credentials" is what the tools built on it call the
result). A consortium of camera makers, software companies, and AI labs
(major names: Adobe, Microsoft, Google, Intel, Nikon, Sony, OpenAI, and
others) agreed on a format for storing the trail inside the media file
itself:

- **What made it.** "Captured with Nikon Z8 at 10:14 AM" — or,
  importantly, "Generated with [some AI tool]".
- **What was done since.** "Cropped," "Color adjusted," "Faces blurred" —
  each recorded as a signed step in an edit history that lives in the file.
- **Whether the trail is intact.** Receipts are tamper-evident: if someone
  edits the file after it was signed, the chain shows the break — which is
  the whole value of signing in the first place.

## How to actually look at a receipt

The universal, free way right now:

1. Save the original image or video file (as it arrived — not a
   screenshot of it — see the big caveat below).
2. Open **contentcredentials.org/verify** in a browser and upload it.
3. Read the trail: you'll see "generated by...," "edited with...," or the
   camera it came from — or the message "no Content Credentials found."
   "No credentials found" is a *nothing* result: many ordinary cameras and
   apps don't attach receipts (yet), and every share hop strips some
   metadata. Absence means "use the other chapters," not "fake."

You'll also meet receipts in the wild, without any tool:

- **Meta ("AI info" labels).** Facebook, Instagram, and Threads put an
  "AI info" marker on content that Meta's systems detect as AI-made or that
  carries credentials saying so. Click it: the explanation card often tells
  you *why* the label exists.
- **YouTube's "altered content" banner** flags videos containing synthetic
  or doctored elements (look under the title or in the description).
- **Camera-native receipts.** Several professional and prosumer cameras
  can attach Content Credentials at the moment of capture — a big deal for
  photojournalism: press photos from those cameras can show they were
  untouched between the scene and you.
- **"About this image" in Google Search** (some regions/searches): search
  the image's origin, when/where it first appeared, and how other pages use
  it — a receipt-adjacent freebie that pairs well with chapter 06.

## The invisible-watermark lane

Some AI tools additionally sign their content *into the pixels* — an
invisible watermark that shares and screenshots can survive. Google's
system is called **SynthID** (with a public detector for images made by
their tools); other labs use similar approaches. Invisible watermarks are a
different lane from C2PA: rather than receipts attached to a file, they're
patterns baked into the pixels themselves. The practical version: a text
label or a detector that says "watermark found — AI" is a strong positive
signal (for AI-made content), while "watermark not found" proves nothing —
only some tools watermark, and it doesn't apply to ordinary photos at all.

## The honest limits (the five-line table)

| Limit | What it means for you |
| --- | --- |
| Screenshots and re-encodes strip receipts | A screenshot of a post hides every credential the post had. Read receipts from the *original file*, whenever you can get it |
| Absence of receipts proves nothing | Most cameras/apps still don't attach them; forwards strip them; a receipt-less photo is normal, not suspicious |
| Presence isn't a magic stamp of truth | A signed chain tells you the *edit history is as claimed* — judge the content itself with chapters 02–05 |
| Labels come from the platforms | Platform "AI info" markers are one signal (their detectors miss plenty, and labeling rules shift). Combining signals beats any single one |
| The ecosystem is young | Tools, labels, and rules keep changing; what you learned here is the stable shape of it: files can carry receipts, and you can read them |

:::tip Where provenance shines (add it to the playbook)
When a viral image is *high stakes* (an event photo making a political or
money-relevant claim) and it's still possible to get the original file, the
receipt check is the best first move in the whole guide: one upload, and
either the trail says "generated" (done — you have certainty), or the trail
says nothing (go do chapters 06 and 08).
:::

One cultural note before we move on: some people feel it's harsh that AI
media carries these labels at all — "all photography edits; why is one kind
marked?" The difference is that a receipt tells you when pixels were
*painted by a model from a sentence* rather than *taken of the world*, and
the two make different promises to a reader. Labels don't judge AI art —
they just stop it from quietly auditioning as news.
