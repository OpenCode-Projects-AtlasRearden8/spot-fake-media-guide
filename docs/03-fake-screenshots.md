# 03: Fake Screenshots: Receipts That Aren't Receipts

A screenshot feels like evidence. It's a picture *of a screen* — of the post,
the text message, the headline, the notification — and pictures of screens
smell official. But a screenshot is just an image, and images are exactly as
easy to fake as everything else in chapter 02. A screenshot proves only one
thing: that this string of pixels exists. Not that the post exists, not that
the text was sent, not that the headline ran.

The fix is to learn to read a screenshot the way a bank teller reads a large
bigger bill: quickly, at the edges, looking for seams.

## The eight screenshot checks

1. **Do the words exist anywhere else?** Type the exact text (in quotation
   marks) into a search engine, then search the platform itself for the
   poster's name. Fake screenshots invent both new posts and new handles.
   If the claim is a quote of a public figure, also search for a
   fact-check or the original video — real speeches get taken out of
   context constantly, and the context is usually a search away.
2. **The pasted-line seam.** Doctored screenshots often have one line of
   text pasted over another screenshot. That line is usually subtly
   different: slightly different font weight, a baseline that doesn't sit
   with the rest, a different pixel density (it looks "softer" or "harder"
   than its neighbors), or — the classic — the paste box edges visible as a
   faint rectangle when you zoom on the background around the words.
3. **The theme and version clues.** A claimed Instagram notification in the
   style of 2019; a "dark mode" app frame with a "light mode" message
   bubble inside it; an Android status bar around an iPhone message bubble;
   an emoji style that no version of the platform uses. Fake compositors get
   the chrome nearly right and the fonts nearly right — and that nearness
   is exactly what rings a bell the moment you zoom.
4. **The clock math.** Time zones play games — a "9:41 PM" notification
   that's simultaneous with a "3:41 PM" status bar; a timestamp that falls
   inside February 30; a "yesterday" post from a weekday that was Monday.
   When handy: a real phone automatically loads its time in the status bar
   from the system; if the status bar and message timestamps disagree with
   the post's story, that's an edit.
5. **The reply logic.** Real conversations thread naturally. Fakes often
   show a "reply" that quotes text slightly different from the original
   message above it, or a reaction count that doesn't fit the number of
   visible names, or a like/follower count that jumps between two panels
   of the same screenshot.
6. **The handle fingerprint.** Real account names get mimicked with
   extra letters, missing underscores, homoglyphs (the Cyrillic "а" that
   looks like "a"), or a different domain after the @. Zoom on the handle
   and compare it, character by character, with the real one on the live
   platform.
7. **"Forwarded many times" labels.** In WhatsApp and similar apps, that
   little banner above a message tells you how many times it has traveled —
   it says nothing about truth, only that this is not its first stop. It's
   a prompt to reach for this checklist, not proof either way.
8. **What the screenshot isn't.** The single biggest tell is
   **absence of the original link**. A screenshot of a post is a photograph
   of a door — the creator could just as easily have sent you the door.
   People crop out handles, timestamps, and URLs all the time for privacy,
   but when a *striking claim* arrives conveniently without pieces you
   could verify, the convenient emptiness is itself one of the tells.

:::note Why even a real screenshot isn't proof
A screenshot of a headline is a photograph of a circle of text under a video
frame of a claim — all real pixels, none of it necessarily the facts. When a
screenshot says something important, click through to the full story or post
itself whenever you can. The original is always more informative than the
photo of the original — and easier to check.
:::

## Reading the "proof" flavor of screenshots

Two sub-flavors deserve their own quick passes:

- **Payment-app receipts.** A screenshot of a bank-transfer or payment-app
  confirmation is a favorite fake. Amounts and names are editable in any
  image editor; the "pending" status is paintable. This is scam territory —
  if someone is showing you money that "already arrived" or "will arrive,"
  follow the checks in *The AI Scam Safety Guide* (its chapter 03 has the
  detailed pass) rather than this guide. One rule belongs here anyway:
  treat in-app balances, not screenshots, as the real balance.
- **Text-message or DM "receipts" between two people.** This flavor gets
  pasted everywhere. Plot check: who benefits from the conversation seeming
  real? Senders control their own copy — a DM where someone *voluntarily*
  incriminates themselves is a genre, not a leak. Same doesn't imply fake,
  but the genre is so common (and the fakes so easy) that "I saw the
  screenshots" should stay in story territory, not fact territory, until
  something corroborates it elsewhere.

## The one-finger workflow (phone)

1. **Zoom** to maximum on the text line you care about; look for the
   pasted-line seam (font weight, baseline, pixel fuzz).
2. **Search the exact text** in quotes; look for the original post.
3. **Search the handle**; compare character by character with the live
   account.
4. **Check the date math** (timestamps vs calendar vs time zones).
5. If it matters, **reverse-image-search the screenshot itself** — doctored
   images often reveal earlier, unedited versions in chapter 06.

:::tip The kindest sentence in a group chat
"I can't find this post anywhere — anyone have the link?" You didn't accuse
anyone. You asked the exact question a fake screenshot cannot survive: the
link request. Fakes usually say "I saw it somewhere" away and the moment
passes; real posts have URLs.
:::
