# 02: AI-Made Images and the Zoom-In Habit

An AI image is generated pixel by pixel from a sentence. The model has never
seen the world directly; it has seen millions of *photos* of the world.
Usually it reassembles those memories convincingly. Sometimes — especially
where images have fiddly structure — the seams show. Not to the eye at
scroll distance. To the eye at **zoom distance**, which is yours.

## The habit itself

When an image matters, **zoom in three times on three places**: once on any
person (start at the hands), once on any text or logo, once at the borders
where the subject meets the background. That's the whole technique. Phone
screens hide the seams; zooming in is like getting to the airport at
daylight — you spot the little lies.

What follows is the ordered list of what to look for. Two tells found is
suspicion. Three or more is "don't share until reverse-searched" (chapter 06).

## The zoom-in tells (use more than one)

1. **Hands and things-hands-are-touching.** Fingers: six, or two joined
   thumbs, or a pinkie holding a coffee cup from inside the cup. Also:
   phones that melt into palms, eyeglasses whose arms multiply, straps that
   pass *through* a wrist, chopsticks phased into ramen.
2. **Anything printed inside the image.** Signs, shirts, packaging,
   name tags, car license plates, calculator displays, newspaper headlines.
   AI is notoriously clumsy with small crisp text: near-words, letters that
   only come into focus as letters when you can't read them, logos that are
   approximately-logo-shaped but miss every detail. If the "evidence" is
   text — a store sign, a banner, a book title — read every letter.
3. **Reflections and mirrors.** A lake should reflect the mountain, not
   swap in a landscape from another photo. Store windows, sunglasses, spoons
   in coffee, eyeglass lenses — check that what's reflected plausibly
   matches the scene. AI often paints reflections from somewhere else
   entirely, leaves one out where physics demands one, or keeps them the
   "wrong side" (hair parted in the mirror backwards is human; a *building*
   next to a person with no counterpart reflection is AI).
4. **Light logic.** One sun per photo. Check that shadows fall in one
   consistent direction, that a face lit from the left isn't casting
   light-colored shadows, that night-window-glow matches the outside
   darkness, that a candle flame lights the wall it stands against.
5. **Background melt.** The further back you zoom, the sloppier generative
   images get: fence posts that merge, a crowd where faces grow doubled or
   melty, bicycles with chain rings welded to nothing, windows that are
   wall-color glass, "trees" that are green texture. AI tends to run out of
   detail budget: the subject is sharp, the world behind it is soup.
6. **Texture mush.** Hair that braids into neck-skin; sweater knits that go
   random; fur that forgets which way it grows; fingertips that blend into
   guitar strings. Human flaws (a loose thread, a scuff) are *detail
   budgets* AI pays out unevenly — half of the sweater flawless, half
   static.
7. **Edge seams around the subject.** Where AI has pasted or regenerated a
   person, look for a faint halo or glow around the outline, railing that
   is *both* in front of and behind the leg, hair edges that shimmer
   against a cluttered background, jewelry pixels invading skin.
8. **The teeth-and-eyes fine print.** Zoom on a smile: teeth may melt
   together, miscount, or have crooked brightness. Eyes: asymmetric
   highlights, irises that don't match both eyes, eyebrow-to-eye distance
   that keeps shifting between crop levels.

## What real photos look like (so you don't accuse real things)

Real phone photos are full of *unhelpful imperfections*: motion blur, noise
in dark corners, one area slightly out of focus, lens smudges, a lamp
blowing out rather than "perfectly." Generated images tend to be *overly
even* — everything crisply believable, nothing coincidentally wrong. That's
a vibe, not proof; but when an image looks a touch too varnished, zoom twice
more before deciding.

Note that big camera-phone processing does a lot of "AI-addon" unification
itself (night mode, portrait blur). So slight smoothness around a subject
happened in 2018 too, in your own camera. This is why one tell is never a
verdict — and why a real photo can show one tell and stay real.

## The verdict ladder (a light one)

| What the zoom shows | What you do |
| --- | --- |
| No tells, ordinary scene from a normal source | Believe freely |
| One fuzzy tell, low resolution, or heavy filter | "Meh" — don't forward; move on unless it matters |
| Two tells on a striking claim | Run a reverse image search before sharing |
| Three tells or AI-look "victim evidence" | Treat the image as an AI suspect; report to the poster kindly or scroll |

Two honest footnotes:

- **Fresh images defeat your eyes.** The newest generators produce images
  with no reachable tells; by the time you find the mismatch, the next
  generator already fixed it. That's what chapters 06–08 are for: tools
  that don't rely on your noticing wrongness.
- **Low resolution hides tells on purpose.** Forwarded images arrive as
  blurry "compressed phone wallpaper," which washes out seams. If someone
  is asking you to believe a striking image and the only copy available is
  tiny, ask for the original. People avoid sending the original for a
  reason, even when the reason is just habit.

:::tip If you notice nothing wrong
Then record your verdict as "no tells found — not proven real." The checklist
and chapter 06 exist for cases where beauty stays suspicious from a single
angle. Suspecting isn't accusing; checking beats both.
:::

## When it's likely, not when it's pretty

Keep the whole toolkit for **decision-shaped images** — image makes you want
to forward, vote, donate, quote, buy, or cry. An image of a sunset with no
claim attached needs no forensic pass; viewing AI sunsets as paintings is
fine and they are often lovely. The zoom-in habit is for when pixels are
carrying a story.
