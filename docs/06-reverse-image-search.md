# 06: Reverse Image Search: The Five-Minute Undo Button

If this guide could teach only one tool, it would be this one. Reverse image
search answers a deceptively big question: **"Where did this image come
from, and what were its earlier lives?"** It turns "I feel like I've seen
this somewhere" into receipts. It is the cure for AI images (often), fake
context (usually), and recycled viral fakes (almost always).

The idea: instead of searching *for* words, you give a search engine an
image, and it shows you web pages containing that same image (or close
relatives). Guess what wins every time: whichever page is the oldest — not
the most colorful, not the loudest — the oldest.

## On a phone (the everyday case)

**Google Lens (Android: usually built in; iPhone: via the Google app or
Chrome):**

1. Long-press (or right-click, on a computer) the image itself.
2. Choose **"Search image with Google Lens"** (wording varies).
3. Read what Lens shows first: it often *finds similar results right away*
   and may put a label like "AI-generated" or source links over the photo
   itself. Then open the matches one by one, and always check the date on
   the page itself (not the thumbnail).

**TinEye:**

1. Save the image (or long-press → copy image).
2. Go to tineye.com and upload it (or paste the image URL).
3. Sort the results by **oldest first**. This one sort is the single most
   clarifying click in this whole guide: it makes the truth legible. A
   "news event happening now" photo that already existed on a blog in 2021
   is now simply a photo from 2021 — case closed.

**Bing and Yandex:** Bing Visual Search (bing.com) and Yandex Images
(yandex.com/images) aren't better than Google; they aren't worse — they are
*different*, and switching engines has saved many a check. Yandex is
distinctly good at **faces**; TinEye is distinctly good at **when did this
exist first**. If one engine says "no results," try one more before
concluding anything.

## Reading results like a pro (the five habits)

1. **Find the oldest match, not the top one.** Sort by date where possible
   (TinEye); on Google, check the date shown *on the source pages*, not the
   engine's thumbnail.
2. **Compare contexts, not just pixels.** The same photo telling three
   different stories — "storm in Indonesia 2019," "flooding today in
   Spain," "the disaster fund that needs your click" — is your shallowfake
   warning. The original story wins; the latest story borrows.
3. **Note the *kind* of site each match lives on.** News agencies, museum
   sites, and event pages point toward real photography. Stock libraries,
   "AI art showcase" sites, and pages that collect known generated images
   point toward fiction. A match on the gallery of an artist who posts
   synthetic work, with the caption "my generation, [date]" — that's a
   receipt for AI.
4. **Search your own crop.** If whole-image search finds nothing, crop a
   distinctive region (the face, the sign, one mountain peak) and search
   that. Edits and border crops break whole-image matching, and faces are
   good crops.
5. **Absence of matches is not proof of realness.** Brand-new AI images
   aren't indexed yet — "no results" is common and means "keep other
   checks," not "beautiful, therefore real."

## The two privacy guardrails

- **Never** use reverse image search on intimate photos (of anyone, but
  especially anyone identifiable). It's a dignity matter and, in some
  places, a legal one — some tools refuse such searches outright anyway.
- Reverse image search sends the image to the engine's servers. That's
  fine for memes and news, but if the image contains sensitive material, use your judgment about what you hand to which service. (General
  "what not to paste where" habits live in *The Privacy Comfort Handbook*.)

## The video keyframe recap

For anything filmed: pause on a bright, distinctive frame, screenshot it,
reverse-search the screenshot. One frame search is usually better than any
"video is fake" hunch or "video detector" tool, since a video's original
scenes usually exist online with their documentation intact. Spoken-word clips can often be
found through their transcript too: search a unique phrase in quotes, and
chapter 08 walks through that kind of check.
